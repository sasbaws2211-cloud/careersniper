The big job boards are where many openings are shown, but they are not where every opening starts. A company usually posts a role on its own career page first, and what reaches LinkedIn or Indeed depends on where that company chooses to advertise. Some roles never go further. Here is how to look at the source.

1. Start with company career pages

Almost every company with more than a few employees has a Careers or Jobs link, usually in the footer of its website. That page is the most direct view of what is open: it is run by the employer, it is the page where your application will end up anyway, and it does not depend on a job board copying the listing.

Make a list of 20 to 30 companies you would genuinely like to work for. Include a few you have not heard much about. Open each career page, and save the ones that have roles close to yours. This takes an hour once and saves you from searching broadly every week.

2. Learn which hiring system a career page uses

Most career pages are not hand-built. They are powered by a hiring system (an applicant tracking system, or ATS) such as Greenhouse, Lever or Ashby, and these systems publish a public board for each company at a predictable address. If you recognise the pattern, you can find a company's board even when the company hides it a few clicks deep. We explain the common ones in Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby: how company job boards work.

3. Search the boards directly

A search engine can search inside one of these boards if you tell it which one. For example:

  • site:jobs.lever.co "product designer" remote
  • site:boards.greenhouse.io "data analyst" London
  • site:jobs.ashbyhq.com "customer success"

This works, but treat the results with care. Search engines keep old pages for a while, so you will meet roles that have already closed. Always check the posting date and whether the company's own page still lists the role.

4. Use a tool that reads many boards at once

Searching one board at a time does not scale. Several tools read many company boards and put the roles in one searchable list. {{ PLATFORM }} is one of them: it reads the public boards of companies that use 11 hiring systems, and lets you filter by country, remote, level and pay. Others take different approaches, and we compare them honestly on our alternatives page. Whichever you use, the useful question is the same: does it show me when a role was last confirmed open?

5. Check that the role is still open before you apply

An application costs you time, so spend a minute first. Find the role on the company's own page, look at how old it is, and see whether the same role keeps reappearing. We list the signs in how to spot a stale or ghost job posting.

6. Apply directly, and keep notes

Applying through the company's own page means your application goes straight into the system the recruiter uses. Keep a simple record of where you applied, the date, and the name of the role, so that you can follow up and avoid applying twice. A spreadsheet is enough, and {{ PLATFORM }} has a tracker built in.

A simple weekly routine

  1. Monday: check your saved company pages and any alerts for new roles.
  2. Midweek: apply to the three or four best matches, with a short note tailored to each role.
  3. Friday: follow up on anything older than a week, and add two or three new companies to your list.

What this will not do

Looking beyond the big boards finds more roles, but it does not guarantee fewer applicants or an easier process. Some companies, especially small ones, do not use any hiring system and post only on their own site or by word of mouth. And a role that is on a career page may be on LinkedIn too. The aim is to see more of what is actually open, earlier, and to spend your effort on roles that are real.
