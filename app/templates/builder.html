How to build a CV that gets read

  1. Check what the country and field expect. In the UK, Ireland, much of Europe, Africa and Asia, “CV” is the normal word for the document you send with a job application. In the US it usually means a longer academic document, and employers there ask for a résumé.
  2. Put the strongest section first. Early-career and academic CVs often start with education. Experienced candidates usually lead with work experience.
  3. Use numbers. “Cut report time from 40 seconds to 6” says more than “improved performance”.
  4. Keep it to two pages for most jobs. Academic and research CVs are longer, because they list publications, grants and teaching.
  5. Match the posting. Use the same names for skills and tools that the job description uses, as long as they are true.

What goes on a CV

A profile, education, work experience and skills are the core. Many CVs add publications, research, teaching, languages and a references line. This builder has all of those. It does not ask for a photo, date of birth or marital status: many employers do not expect them, and they can lead to unfair treatment. Check what is normal where you are applying.

How to build a résumé that gets read

  1. Lead with your most relevant experience. Recruiters skim. Put the roles and results that match the job first.
  2. Write results, not duties. Start each point with what you did, and add a number where you honestly can.
  3. Keep it to one page if you can, two at most. Cut old or unrelated roles before you shrink the text.
  4. Reuse the posting’s words. If the job asks for “stakeholder management” and you have done it, say so in those words.
  5. Keep the layout plain. Fancy columns, icons and photos can confuse the software that reads résumés.

What “ATS-friendly” means here

Many employers run applications through an applicant tracking system (ATS) before a person looks at them. The three layouts here are one column with standard section headings, real selectable text, and no tables, icons or photos. The PDF contains text, not a picture of text, and the Word file uses real headings and bullet lists. That helps, but no tool can promise how every employer’s system will read a file.
